Hearsay exceptions and exclusions

Three batteries, easy to hard: the 801(d) exclusions, the 803 exceptions that work with any declarant, and the 804 exceptions that need an unavailable one. Each section opens with a compact rule card, then its battery. The final at the end is closed book and mixes everything.

Section 1 · Not hearsay at all

The 801(d) exclusions — statements that are not hearsay

Rule 801(d)(2) — an opposing party’s statement

Offered against a party, made by their side

A statement is not hearsay if it is offered against an opposing party and:

  • (A) the party made it (individual or representative capacity) — no oath, no personal-knowledge screen, and it did not have to hurt them when they said it;
  • (B) the party manifested that it adopted or believed it — words, conduct, or a silence where a denial would be natural;
  • (C) a person the party authorized to speak on the subject made it;
  • (D) the party’s agent or employee made it, on a matter within the scope of that relationship, while it existed;
  • (E) a coconspirator made it, during and in furtherance of the conspiracy.

Since Dec 2024 the rule adds: if a party’s claim, defense, or potential liability is directly derived from a declarant or the declarant’s principal, a statement admissible against the declarant or principal is admissible against the party.

Worked example. A store manager, still employed, tells a customer “we mopped that aisle and forgot the sign.” The slip-and-fall plaintiff offers it against the store: (D) — employee, matter within the scope, made during employment. Admissible.

Rule 801(d)(1) — a testifying witness’s prior statement

The declarant is on the stand and can be crossed

  • (A) a prior inconsistent statement counts for its truth only if it was given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, other proceeding, or deposition;
  • (B) a prior consistent statement comes in to rebut a charge of recent fabrication or improper motive — and to do that rebutting, it must have been made before the motive arose — or to rehabilitate credibility attacked on another ground;
  • (C) a prior identification of a person the witness perceived earlier — no oath required.

Worked example. A witness told police the robber wore a green jacket; on the stand she says gray. The unsworn police statement can impeach her, but it is not substantive evidence — (A)’s oath requirement fails.

Section 2 · Hearsay, but admissible anyway

The 803 exceptions — declarant availability doesn’t matter

Rule 803 — the exceptions you can use with any declarant

It doesn’t matter where the declarant is, or whether they could testify

  • (1) Present sense impression — describes an event or condition, made while or immediately after perceiving it.
  • (2) Excited utterance — relates to a startling event, made while still under the stress of the excitement it caused. The clock here is the stress, not the minutes.
  • (3) Then-existing state of mind or condition — “I’m scared,” “my leg hurts,” “I’m going to Denver Friday.” A stated plan can prove the person later acted on it. But not a statement of memory or belief offered to prove the fact remembered.
  • (4) Medical diagnosis or treatment — made for diagnosis or treatment and pertinent to it: symptoms, history, cause. Who was at fault usually isn’t pertinent.
  • (6) Business records — made at or near the time by someone with knowledge, kept in the course of a regularly conducted activity, making the record a regular practice — unless the opponent shows the source or circumstances indicate a lack of trustworthiness.
  • (8) Public records — a public office’s activities and observations, but in a criminal case the prosecution cannot use matters observed by law enforcement, or investigative findings, against the defendant.
  • 805 — hearsay within hearsay — a statement quoted inside a record (or inside another statement) is its own layer, and every layer needs its own exclusion or exception.

Worked example. “That truck is drifting out of its lane” — said while watching it happen — is a present sense impression. The same sentence in a calm phone call that evening is just a story about the afternoon.

Section 3 · Only when the declarant is gone

The 804 exceptions — you must show the declarant is unavailable first

Rule 804(a) — what “unavailable” means

Five ways in, one way that never counts

  • A privilege applies and the court so rules · the declarant refuses to testify despite a court order · the declarant testifies to not remembering the subject · death, or a then-existing infirmity or illness · absent from trial and beyond the reach of process or other reasonable means.
  • Never: a declarant is not unavailable if the statement’s proponent procured or wrongfully caused the absence to prevent the testimony.

Rule 804(b) — the exceptions unavailability unlocks

Former testimony, dying declarations, statements against interest, forfeiture

  • (1) Former testimony — given at a trial, hearing, or deposition, offered against a party who had (or, in a civil case, whose predecessor in interest had) an opportunity and similar motive to develop it.
  • (2) Dying declaration — only in a homicide prosecution or a civil case: a statement about the cause or circumstances of what the declarant believed to be imminent death. The declarant must be unavailable but need not have died.
  • (3) Statement against interest — so contrary to the declarant’s pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest that a reasonable person would not have said it unless true. If it exposes the declarant to criminal liability and is offered in a criminal case, it also needs corroborating circumstances that clearly indicate trustworthiness — and since Dec 2024 the court must consider the totality of the circumstances under which it was made and any evidence that supports or undermines it.
  • (6) Forfeiture — offered against a party who wrongfully caused the declarant’s unavailability, intending exactly that.

Worked example. A witness testifies that she has no memory of the night at all: unavailable, and 804(b) opens. A witness whose memory revives once she reads her old deposition: available, and it stays shut.

Quick Reference

801(d)(2) — opposing party’s statementNot hearsay when offered against a party and made by the party (A), adopted by them (B), by an authorized speaker (C), by an agent or employee on a matter within the scope and during the relationship (D), or by a coconspirator during and in furtherance of the conspiracy (E).Only works offered against that party — a party can never offer their own statement through it. No oath, no personal knowledge, no against-interest showing needed.
801(d)(2), derived liability (Dec 2024)If a party’s claim, defense, or potential liability directly derives from a declarant or the declarant’s principal, a statement admissible against the declarant or principal is admissible against the party.Think successors and derivative claims — the statement follows the liability.
801(d)(1) — testifying witness’s prior statementDeclarant testifies and is subject to cross about it. (A) prior inconsistent — for its truth only if given under penalty of perjury at a proceeding or deposition. (B) prior consistent — to rebut fabrication or improper-motive charges, only if made before the motive arose, or to rehabilitate. (C) prior identification — no oath needed.An unsworn prior inconsistent statement still impeaches; it just isn’t substantive evidence. Faded memory on the stand doesn’t defeat “subject to cross.”
803(1) — present sense impressionDescribes an event or condition, made while perceiving it or immediately after.“Immediately” means moments, not minutes of reflection.
803(2) — excited utteranceRelates to a startling event, made while under the stress of the excitement it caused.The test is the stress, not the stopwatch — but calm narration fails however soon it comes.
803(3) — then-existing state of mindCurrent feelings, pain, intent, plan. A stated plan can prove the declarant later acted on it.Never a statement of memory or belief to prove the fact remembered — check every 803(3) offer against this carve-out first.
803(4) — medical diagnosis or treatmentMade for — and pertinent to — diagnosis or treatment: symptoms, history, general cause.“A crate fell on my foot” is pertinent. “It was Dave’s fault” is not.
803(6) — business recordsMade at or near the time, by or from someone with knowledge, kept in the regular course, making the record a regular practice, shown by a custodian or certification.The opponent bears the burden of showing untrustworthiness — but records built for litigation lose the presumption.
803(8) — public recordsA public office’s activities, legally required observations, and (in civil cases and against the government) investigative findings.In criminal cases, law-enforcement observations and investigative findings don’t come in against the defendant — and 803(6) can’t rescue them.
805 — hearsay within hearsayEach layer needs its own exclusion or exception.A business record containing a customer’s statement needs a rule for the record and a rule for the customer.
804(a) — unavailabilityPrivilege · refusal despite court order · lack of memory · death or infirmity · absence beyond process or other reasonable means.Never available to a proponent who procured the absence to prevent testimony.
804(b)(1) — former testimonyTestimony from a trial, hearing, or deposition, offered against a party who had — or in a civil case whose predecessor in interest had — an opportunity and similar motive to develop it.Grand jury testimony offered against a defendant fails: the defense had no opportunity at all.
804(b)(2) — dying declarationHomicide prosecution or civil case only. Cause or circumstances of what the declarant believed to be imminent death.The declarant must be unavailable but need not have died. Wrong case type kills it entirely.
804(b)(3) — statement against interestSo contrary to pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest that a reasonable person would only say it if true. Penal-interest statements in criminal cases need corroborating circumstances clearly indicating trustworthiness; the court considers the totality of circumstances under which the statement was made and any evidence that supports or undermines it (Dec 2024).Distinguish 801(d)(2)(A): a party’s own statement needs no unavailability and no against-interest showing.
804(b)(6) — forfeiture by wrongdoingOffered against a party who wrongfully caused — or acquiesced in causing — the declarant’s unavailability, intending that result.Intent to silence is an element; ordinary wrongdoing that happens to silence isn’t enough.
