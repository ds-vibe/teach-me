The Offside Rule

A cross comes in. The striker is two strides beyond the last defender when his team-mate shoots, and the ball flies straight into the net. He never touches it. Half the pub says the goal cannot stand; the assistant referee keeps the flag down. Law 11 settles arguments like this one — but it is not the version people repeat. This page teaches you to make the call yourself, from your sofa, before the replay does.

§1 of 3 · Law 11.1

The offside position

Offside exists to make goal-hanging worthless. Without it, the smart plan is to park a striker on the goalkeeper's toes and hit every ball long. The law forces attackers to stay connected to the play, and every clause in it serves that one job. The first half of the job is a definition: who counts as ahead of the play at all?

The moment that counts

Every offside decision starts with a freeze-frame. The instant a team-mate plays or touches the ball, the picture is taken: where was everyone at that exact moment? Nothing that happens while the ball travels is in the picture. A striker who looks two yards offside when the ball reaches him may have been onside in the frame that matters — which is why the broadcast draws its lines at the moment of the pass, not the moment the striker meets the ball.

The two markers

In that frozen frame the law compares the striker to two markers: the ball, and the second-last opponent. He is in an offside position only if part of him is nearer to the opponents' goal line than both.

Note what the law counts: opponents, not defenders. The goalkeeper is simply one of the eleven. On an ordinary afternoon the keeper is the last opponent, back on his line, so the second-last opponent is the last outfield defender — which is where the pub's "last defender" came from. The pub memorised the usual picture; the law states a test, and the two come apart the moment the keeper leaves his goal.

The ball marker works the same way: a striker level with or behind the ball is onside, however few opponents are ahead of him. You cannot be offside on a pass that travels backwards to you.

Level is onside

If he is level with the second-last opponent — or level with the last two — he is not in an offside position. The law says nearer, and level is not nearer. Exactly level goes to the attacker, every time. But anything genuinely nearer, however small, fails the test: the replay lines you see on television exist to find margins the eye cannot.

Own half, and which body parts count

Two boundaries finish the definition. First, there is no offside in your own half: some part of head, body or feet must be in the opponents' half, and the halfway line itself does not count as the opponents' half. Second, hands and arms are never measured — for attackers, defenders and goalkeepers alike. The usual explanation: you may not score with your arm, so the law does not measure you by it. For offside, the arm ends at the bottom of the armpit.

Move the players — watch the line

The dashed line sits at the second-last opponent or the ball, whichever is nearer the goal line. The keeper stays on his line here, so he is the last opponent and the moving defender is the second-last.

Law 11.1 · Offside position

What the law says

Two markers, one frozen moment — and level goes to the attacker.

IFAB Law 11 ↗

What it measures — one thing only: had he already beaten both the ball and the defence at the moment his team played it?

A player is in an offside position if:

  • any part of the head, body or feet is in the opponents’ half (excluding the halfway line) and
  • any part of the head, body or feet is nearer to the opponents’ goal line than both the ball and the second-last opponent

The hands and arms of all players, including the goalkeepers, are not considered. For the purposes of determining offside, the upper boundary of the arm is in line with the bottom of the armpit.

A player is not in an offside position if level with the: second-last opponent or last two opponents

Worked call — At the delivery, the replay line shows the striker's lead foot a boot-length nearer the goal line than the second-last opponent's knee, with the ball behind them both. Nearer than both markers, in the opponents' half: offside position.

§2 of 3 · Law 11.2

When a position becomes an offence

The law waits to see what he does. A player in an offside position when the ball is played is penalised only if he becomes involved in active play — and involvement takes exactly three forms.

1 · Touching the ball

The law calls this interfering with play: playing or touching a ball that a team-mate played or touched. One brush of the boot is enough. It does not need to be a deliberate pass to him — a mishit shot that trickles his way counts the same.

2 · Affecting an opponent, without touching the ball

The second form needs no touch at all: blocking an opponent's line of vision — the keeper's, usually; challenging an opponent for the ball; lunging at a ball close to him when the attempt puts an opponent off; or any obvious movement that clearly affects an opponent's ability to play it. This is the form the pub misses most. The striker who leaps over a cross and never makes contact can still be offside — if his leap froze the keeper, he was involved.

3 · Profiting from a rebound or a save

The third form is called gaining an advantage. He was in an offside position at the shot; the ball comes back off the post, the crossbar, a match official or an opponent — or any opponent deliberately saves it — and he plays it. The original position still counts against him. A save never resets it.

One boundary is worth knowing about, because it decides real goals: if an opponent deliberately plays the ball — a controlled pass or clearance, not a save or a stretch to intercept — the attacker's old position stops counting and play starts fresh. Exactly where a deliberate play ends and a deflection begins has its own body of rulings, and it sits outside this lesson.

The sanction, in every case: an indirect free kick from where the involvement happened — which can even be inside the attacker's own half, if that is where he came back to touch the ball.

Law 11.2 · Offside offence

What the law says

Position plus involvement — and involvement has three forms.

IFAB Law 11 ↗

Why it reads this way — the game only punishes the player who affects it. Law 11.1 opens with the sentence the pub forgets: “It is not an offence to be in an offside position.”

A player in an offside position at the moment the ball is played or touched* by a team-mate is only penalised on becoming involved in active play by:

  • interfering with play by playing or touching a ball passed or touched by a team-mate or
  • interfering with an opponent by: [four ways — full text below] or
  • gaining an advantage by playing the ball or interfering with an opponent when it has: rebounded or been deflected off the goalpost, crossbar, match official or an opponent · been deliberately saved by any opponent
Full text: the four ways of interfering with an opponent, and the footnote

interfering with an opponent by:

  • preventing an opponent from playing or being able to play the ball by clearly obstructing the opponent’s line of vision or
  • challenging an opponent for the ball or
  • clearly attempting to play a ball which is close when this action impacts on an opponent or
  • making an obvious action which clearly impacts on the ability of an opponent to play the ball

*The first point of contact of the ‘play’ or ‘touch’ of the ball should be used; however, when the ball is thrown by the goalkeeper, the last point of contact should be used.

Worked call — Shot from the edge of the box with a striker a stride offside at the strike; the ball thumps back off the crossbar and he sweeps it in. Form three: he gained an advantage from the rebound. Offside — indirect free kick where he swept it.

§3 of 3 · Law 11.3

The exempt restarts, and the full check

Three restarts, exempt by name

Receive the ball directly from a goal kick, a throw-in or a corner kick, and there is no offside offence — however far beyond the defence you stood. The list is closed. It is those three, and nothing else: a free kick is ordinary play as far as Law 11 is concerned, and so is a dropped ball.

"Directly" is the working word. The exemption covers the first delivery only; the moment a team-mate touches the ball after the restart, ordinary offside is back on.

Law 11.3 · No offence

What the law says

Three exemptions — first delivery only.

IFAB Law 11 ↗

There is no offside offence if a player receives the ball directly from:

  • a goal kick
  • a throw-in
  • a corner kick

Worked call — A corner is swung to the back post, where a striker waits with only the keeper near him; he heads in. Direct from a corner: no offence, goal stands.

The full check

The main test compresses to three questions, asked in order. As a list:

  1. Frozen at the team-mate's touch — was he in an offside position?
  2. Was that touch anything other than a goal kick, throw-in or corner?
  3. Did he get involved — touch the ball, affect an opponent, or profit from a rebound or save?

Three yeses make the offence: indirect free kick where the involvement happened. Any no, anywhere, and play goes on.

What this lesson does not teach

  • How VAR reviews offside — the review protocol and semi-automated tracking are their own subject.
  • The deliberate-play boundary — where an opponent's "deliberate play" ends and a "deflection" begins decides real goals and has its own body of rulings; this lesson only tells you the boundary exists.
  • The history of Law 11 — the rule has been rewritten many times; none of that is needed to call a match today.
  • Players leaving the field — defenders or attackers stepping off the pitch trigger special positioning rules, left out here.

One thing that is not a limit: Law 11 is written by IFAB and applies worldwide. The same text governs a World Cup final and a Sunday league match. Source: IFAB Laws of the Game 2026/27, the edition in force this season (Law 11 unchanged from 2025/26).
